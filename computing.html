Notes from 9 years at the quantum–classical boundary.

Where protocols keep turning up in hardware they're not built for.

the open question

Digital computing has clear rules. Everything else is still fuzzy.

We know how digital computers scale, where they break, and what they cost. Analog, quantum, stochastic, in-memory, and other compute paradigms all promise something better, but where or when any of them actually excel isn't always clear. Most claims rest on one benchmark, one problem size, or a comparison against a single classical method.

Much of my research circles this question — what a given physical system can really compute, and how you'd know.

the boundary moves

Where quantum ends and classical begins isn't fixed.

Google's 2019 quantum supremacy experiment drew a line: a task its processor finished in minutes, estimated to take a classical supercomputer thousands of years. Better classical simulation methods then cut that estimate dramatically. The line didn't vanish, but it moved, and it will move again.

Results like the ones above move it too, from the classical side — every time an ordinary system reproduces something that looked uniquely quantum.

what comes out

Right now it's a big jumble, and we're waiting to see what comes out of it.

Nobody has sorted out which mix of analog, quantum, stochastic and digital processing ends up useful, or for what. Getting there takes people willing to carry ideas across substrates and test them honestly, including reporting when something doesn't hold up. That's the work I like, and it's what I try to explain on my channel.
